A Note to You
Welcome

Dear Youth and Family,

You are reading this because something hard has happened, and someone decided that coming here might help. We want you to know, before anything else, that we understand how heavy that is. Being away from home, away from the people you love, in a place with rules you didn't choose — that is a lot to carry. We see you carrying it.

This handbook is yours. It belongs to you while you are here, and you can take it with you when you leave. It explains your rights, the rules of the house, and what to expect each day. It also explains the work we hope to do together — what we call your Recovery Journey. The journey has four parts: Recognition (noticing where you are), Making Peace (letting go of the story that you are broken), Commitment (choosing to do the work), and Proper Action (practicing new ways, a little at a time, in real life).

We want to be clear about something: the journey is yours, not ours. We are not ranking you. We are not giving you points. We are not moving you up or down. You move through these stages at your own pace, in your own way. Sometimes you will be in Stage 1 for one part of your life and Stage 3 for another. That is normal. Our job is to walk with you, not to grade you.

Please read this handbook with a staff member in your first few days. Ask questions. If something does not make sense, ask again. If something feels wrong, tell us — or tell one of the independent advocacy organizations listed on the cover. You have the right to be safe here. You have the right to be heard. You have the right to do the hard work of growing up without being harmed in the process.

We are honored that you and your family have trusted us with this time. We will not take that trust lightly.

With warmth,

Our Promise to You
The Core Principle
“We honor the trust of our children and families, thus we shall not harm during treatment or the use of and consequences.” — Well Spring Intervention LLC

This is the promise we make to every youth and family who comes through our doors. It is not a slogan. It is the rule we measure every other rule against. If a policy, a consequence, or a staff action would cause you harm, it is wrong — even if it is technically allowed. We will choose your safety over our convenience, every time.

In practical terms, this means we will never use cruel, unusual, or corporal punishment. We will never hit you, spank you, or threaten your body. We will never take away food, sleep, clothing, or shelter to teach you a lesson. We will never deny you school, religious practice, or contact with your family as a consequence for behavior. These are not just our policies — they are your rights, and we take them seriously.

We also will not use seclusion (locking you in a room alone) or physical restraint (holding you down) except in true emergencies — when there is imminent danger to you or someone else, and only for as long as that danger lasts. Even then, it is not a punishment. It is a safety measure, used by trained staff, with a doctor's order, and reviewed afterwards by your treatment team. We are working toward a model called Ukeru — a trauma-informed approach that uses comfort and redirection instead of restraint. North Carolina began piloting this in PRTFs in August 2025, and we are committed to that direction.

If a staff member ever does something that feels like harm — physical, emotional, or sexual — you have the right to tell anyone, including the independent advocacy organizations on the cover of this handbook. You will not get in trouble for telling. We mean that.

For Families
Why We Made This Promise
We built our core principle on a simple belief: children heal in relationship, not in fear. Many of the youth who come to us have already survived things no child should have to survive — abuse, loss, disruption, the deep ache of being told (in words or in silence) that they are too much, too broken, too hard. The last thing they need is a treatment setting that adds more harm to that pile. So we made a deliberate choice: comfort over control, conversation over coercion, relationship over compliance. We are working toward the Ukeru model — a trauma-informed approach that uses comfort and redirection instead of restraint. We treat family contact as a right, not a privilege to be earned. And we hold ourselves to a standard that asks not just “is this allowed?” but “is this kind?”
Section 1
Your Rights & How to File a Grievance

You keep your rights when you come here. Being in treatment does not make you less of a person. The State of North Carolina guarantees certain rights to every resident of a licensed facility, and we add a few of our own on top. Here are the most important ones, in plain language.

Your 12 Core Rights
  • Dignity and respect. Staff must call you by your preferred name, speak to you respectfully, and respect your privacy.
  • A humane environment. Clean, safe, comfortable living space. Heating, cooling, lighting, and ventilation that work.
  • Uncensored communication. You may send and receive mail and make phone calls to family, attorneys, courts, DHHS, and Disability Rights NC without staff listening in or reading your mail (we may open mail in your presence for contraband inspection only).
  • Religious practice. You may practice the religion of your choice — or no religion at all. No one may pressure you to participate in religious activities.
  • Education. You have the right to attend school and continue your education. We will work with your home school district to keep you on track.
  • Refusal of medication. You have the right to refuse medication to the extent permitted by law. If you refuse, your treatment team will talk with you and your guardian about what that means for your care.
  • Attorney access. You may speak with an attorney at any time, privately, at your own expense or through court-appointed counsel.
  • Private physician. You may see a private physician of your choice at your own expense, with reasonable notice.
  • Personal possessions. You may keep reasonable personal items, including clothing, photos, religious items, and money (held securely for you).
  • Visitation. You may receive visits from family and approved visitors at scheduled times. Family contact is a right, not a privilege to be earned.
  • File a grievance. You may complain about anything — staff, food, rules, other residents, your treatment — without fear of retaliation.
  • Contact advocacy organizations. You may contact Disability Rights NC and The Joint Commission at any time. Staff must let you use the phone privately to call them.
How to File a Grievance — 5 Steps
  1. Tell a staff member. Any staff member. Start with whoever you feel safest with. They will listen and try to help fix it informally first.
  2. Write it down. If talking doesn't solve it (or if you'd rather start on paper), ask for a grievance form. Staff will help you fill it out if you want help. You can also just write a note.
  3. Submit it. Give it to any staff member, or drop it in the locked grievance box (located in the common area). Anonymous grievances are accepted, though we may not be able to respond without a name.
  4. Get a response. You will receive a written response, usually within 5 business days. If the grievance is against a specific staff member, you can ask to speak with their supervisor instead.
  5. Escalate if needed. If you're not satisfied, you can ask for the Program Director, then the Clinical Director, then the Executive Director. At any step, you can also contact the independent organizations below.

Section 2
The 5 Cardinal Rules

These are the five non-negotiable safety rules of our community. They apply to everyone — youth and staff. Breaking a Cardinal Rule doesn't mean you're bad. It means we need to talk about what's underneath the behavior, and we need to make a plan together to keep everyone safe.

1
No Physical Aggression
No hitting, pushing, kicking, biting, grabbing, throwing things at people, or threatening to hurt someone. If you feel like you might hurt someone, tell staff immediately — we will help you cool down before anything happens. You will not get in trouble for asking for help.
2
No Sexual Contact
No sexual behavior with other residents or anyone else — including touching, exposure, suggestive language, or asking others for sexual contact. This protects everyone's safety and dignity. If someone touches you or makes you uncomfortable, tell any staff member right away.
3
No Absconding (Running Away)
Do not leave the facility or a supervised activity without staff permission. If you feel like running, tell a staff member — we will listen to what's making you feel that way and help you figure out a safer option. Running away puts you in real danger, and we care about your safety.
4
No Gang Activity
No gang signs, gang colors, recruiting, intimidation, or gang-related writing. This keeps the community focused on treatment and keeps you safe from outside pressures. If gang affiliation is part of your history, we will talk about it in therapy — not punish you for it.
5
No Name-Calling Based on Identity
No slurs or name-calling based on race, ethnicity, religion, gender, sexual orientation, disability, or body size. Everyone here deserves to be treated with basic respect. Disagreeing is okay; demeaning someone for who they are is not.

If you break a Cardinal Rule, staff will talk with you, your treatment team will meet to make a safety plan, and there may be increased supervision or temporary precautions (like a sharps precaution if you've hurt yourself). These are safety measures, not punishments. They end as soon as it's safe for them to end. Your family contact, meals, sleep, school, and therapy continue regardless.

For Families
How Cardinal Rules Protect Your Child
The 5 Cardinal Rules translate our no-harm principle into youth-friendly, memorable language. They are based on the KidsPeace framework used at many residential treatment facilities nationwide. When a Cardinal Rule is broken, our response is safety-first — not punishment-first. We talk with your child about what's underneath the behavior, we make a plan together, and we never respond by taking away meals, sleep, school, family contact, or dignity. This approach is consistent with the 2014 AACAP and Building Bridges Initiative position papers, which caution against point-and-level systems that restrict family contact or delay discharge. The short version: your child will be held accountable and cared for at the same time. Those two things are not in tension here.
Section 3
Your Recovery Journey

This is the heart of how we think about your time here. We do not use a level system. We do not give you points. We do not move you up or demote you. Instead, we use a four-stage framework — a Recovery Journey — to describe the personal work of healing. You move through these stages at your own pace. Sometimes you'll be in one stage for one part of your life and a different stage for another. That is normal. Staff are not ranking you — we are walking with you.

Stage 1 Recognition
“State of life” — noticing where you are right now, honestly, without judgment.
This is the beginning. It's not about admitting you were wrong or accepting a label. It's about looking at your life — the patterns, the things that have happened to you, the things you've done, the things you've been carrying — and saying, “This is where I am.” Without shame. Without denial. Just honesty.
What this can look like: Talking honestly in therapy. Naming what's hard. Letting staff help. Noticing your own patterns. Showing up to groups even when you don't feel like it.
Stage 2 Making Peace
“Separation from negative self-view and becoming self-positive via normalization of struggle.”
A lot of us grow up believing we are broken, bad, or unlovable because of what happened to us or what we did. Making peace means learning to separate who you are from what happened to you. Struggle is human. You are not broken. You are not your worst moment. This stage is about learning to be on your own side.
What this can look like: Stopping negative self-talk. Being kind to yourself when you mess up. Talking back to the voice that says you're not enough. Letting yourself feel proud of small things. Accepting that hard feelings are part of being human.
Stage 3 Commitment
“To the work of self-realization.”
At some point, healing stops being something other people want for you and becomes something you want for yourself. That's commitment. It doesn't mean you're suddenly motivated all the time. It means you've decided — even on the hard days, even when you're tired — that this work matters. You keep showing up.
What this can look like: Setting your own treatment goals. Engaging in therapy instead of just attending. Trying new coping skills even when the old ones feel easier. Talking about hard things without being prompted.
Stage 4 Proper Action
“Maintaining a realistic adjustment in behavior and perspective.”
This is where the work meets real life. Proper action isn't about being perfect. It's about practicing new ways of handling things — at the dinner table, in a disagreement with a peer, when you feel overwhelmed, when someone disappoints you. Small, realistic changes, sustained over time. Not a transformed person. A practicing one.
What this can look like: Using a coping skill before things escalate. Repairing a relationship after a conflict. Speaking up for yourself respectfully. Trying again after a setback. Planning for what comes next.

You will notice we did not assign time frames to these stages. That is intentional. Some youth move through Recognition in a week; others take months. Some skip back and forth between Making Peace and Commitment for a long time. That is not failure — that is what healing actually looks like. Your treatment team will talk with you about which stage you feel you're in, and what would help you take the next step when you're ready.

Section 4
Behavior — On the Mark, Near the Mark, Off the Mark

Instead of green/yellow/red lights or points and levels, we use three simple categories to talk about behavior: On the Mark, Near the Mark, and Off the Mark. Think of it like archery — you're aiming for something, and your behavior either hits the target, gets close, or misses. The point isn't to be perfect. The point is to notice where you are and adjust.

On On the Mark
Behavior that lines up with your treatment goals and the community's safety. You're using skills, participating, treating people with respect, and following the daily structure.
Examples: Using a coping skill when upset. Attending groups. Speaking respectfully even when frustrated. Following the schedule. Helping a peer. Completing chores without being reminded.
Staff response: Acknowledgment, encouragement, natural privileges continue. We notice. We don't make a big deal out of it — this is just how things work when they're working.
Near Near the Mark
Behavior that's starting to drift but isn't unsafe. You're not breaking Cardinal Rules, but you're also not at your best. This is the early-warning zone — a chance to adjust before things escalate.
Examples: Avoiding a group. Snapping at a peer. Skipping a chore. Rolling your eyes. Withdrawing from conversation. Being short with staff.
Staff response: Verbal redirection. “Take 5” offered (you can ask for time away to reset). A conversation about what's underneath the behavior. No formal consequence — just a check-in.
Off Off the Mark
Behavior that is unsafe, breaks a Cardinal Rule, or seriously disrupts the community. This is when we need to act — not to punish you, but to keep everyone safe and help you get back on track.
Examples: Physical aggression. Running away. Refusing to come back. Property destruction. Sexual contact. Gang activity. Name-calling based on identity.
Staff response: Safety intervention. Treatment team conversation. Possible temporary precautions (sharps, elopement, etc.) — never as punishment, always as safety measures, reviewed regularly, and ended as soon as it's safe.
How We Respond

When you're off the mark, our first question is “What happened to you?” — not “What's wrong with you?” Behavior is communication. We try to understand what you're telling us before we decide what to do next.

Redirection always comes before consequence. Staff will give you three prompts before assigning a formal consequence during your first weeks here. As you grow, you'll help decide what consequences make sense for you — we believe consequences should be logical, restorative, and short. Repair the harm. Replace what was damaged. Reflect on what happened. Move forward.

We will never use group punishment — if one youth breaks a rule, the whole group doesn't lose privileges. We will never take away family contact, meals, sleep, or education as a consequence. We will never use physical restraint or seclusion except in true emergencies, when there is imminent danger, and only for as long as the danger lasts.

Consequences expire. Any restriction of privileges ends within 30 days unless it is reviewed and renewed in writing by your treatment team — with you present and your input recorded.

For Families
Why We Don't Use Points and Levels
Point-and-level systems — where youth “earn” privileges by accumulating points and “lose” them by sliding down a level — are common in residential care, but the research is clear: they tend not to work the way people hope. The 2014 AACAP and Building Bridges Initiative position papers caution that these systems can be “misguided, ineffective, discriminatory, and potentially harmful” when they apply universally rather than individually, restrict family contact as a consequence, or delay discharge. Our On/Near/Off the Mark framework is different. We tie behavior responses to your child's individualized treatment goals, we never restrict family contact as a privilege, and we never use “demotion” punitively. The Healing Lodge of the Seven Nations puts it plainly: “No consequences should ever place a child at risk of injury.” That is the gold standard we follow.

Section 6
Staying Safe — Supervision, Searches & Contraband
How We Keep You Safe

Safety is the foundation. If you don't feel safe, you can't do the work of healing. Our safety model is built on three things: continuous supervision, awake overnight staff, and a 2:4 staffing ratio (at least 2 staff for every 1 to 4 youth, 24/7/365).

During the day, staff maintain line-of-sight supervision. That means we know where you are at all times — not because we're spying on you, but because keeping you safe is our job. If you need privacy (in the bathroom, in your room for a few minutes), we'll give you space, but we'll still know you're okay.

At night, awake staff check on you every 15 minutes while you sleep. They don't wake you up — they just make sure you're breathing, safe, and comfortable. If you have a nightmare, feel sick, or just need to talk, you can tell them. They are not there to enforce rules at night. They're there to keep you safe.

If you ever feel unsafe — with a peer, with a staff member, with a visitor, or even just inside yourself — tell any staff member immediately. You will not get in trouble for telling. If you don't feel safe telling staff, you can call Disability Rights NC (1-877-235-4210) or the Joint Commission (1-800-994-6610) from any phone, privately.

Searches & Contraband
You will never be subjected to a strip search.
This is non-negotiable.

We do searches for safety — yours and everyone else's. We do them with dignity. We document every search with the date, time, staff involved, reason, items found, and your response. Here's what searches look like:

  • Admission search. When you arrive, a same-gender staff member will go through your belongings with you present. We're looking for contraband — weapons, drugs, lighters, sharp objects, anything unsafe. We'll set aside anything you can't keep and store it securely for you to take when you leave.
  • Room searches. Sometimes staff will search your room — usually when there's reasonable suspicion of contraband or a safety concern. We'll tell you why (unless doing so would create a safety risk). Your personal items are handled with care. Anything confiscated is documented.
  • Pat-down searches. Only when there's a specific safety concern (like reasonable suspicion you're carrying a weapon). Done by a same-gender staff member, in a private setting, with a witness. Documented in detail.

The list of items not allowed in the facility (contraband) is in the Family Resources section. The short version: weapons, drugs/alcohol, fire-starting items, sharp objects, medications not in their original container, electronics with cameras, and anything that promotes self-harm or gang affiliation.

For Families
Searches — Our Higher Standard
Every search we do is reasonable, dignified, and documented. And then we go further: strip searches of minors are prohibited in our facility, full stop. This is non-negotiable. We adopted this policy from the New Haven (CA) model because we believe no child should be asked to remove their clothing in the name of safety — there are always better ways to keep a community safe. All searches are logged in a searchable register, our Qualified Professional reviews search logs weekly, and staff are trained annually on trauma-informed search procedures — including the lasting impact that being searched can have on a child who has survived prior trauma. If your child comes home and tells you about a search that doesn't sit right, we want to know.
Section 7
Communication & Visitation

You have the right to stay connected to the people who matter to you. You have the right to uncensored communication by mail and telephone with your family, your attorney, the courts, the Department of Health and Human Services, and Disability Rights NC.

Phone Calls

You'll have scheduled phone times each day (usually evening). If something urgent comes up — a family emergency, a court date, a crisis — you can ask staff to use the phone at any time. Calls with your attorney or Disability Rights NC are always allowed, privately, without staff listening in. We maintain an approved contact list with your guardian's input; clinical holds on contact with specific individuals happen only with guardian consent or a court order.

Mail

You may send and receive mail. Outgoing mail goes out the same day if given to staff before 3 PM. Incoming mail is given to you unopened, except staff may open mail in your presence for contraband inspection only. Reading your mail without your consent is not allowed. If staff are concerned about something in a letter, they'll talk with you about it — not read it secretly.

Visitation

Family visits are encouraged. Approved visitors are listed on your contact list, which your guardian helps build. Visiting hours are typically Saturday and Sunday afternoons, plus a weekday evening. Therapeutic passes and TTVs (therapeutic temporary visits) — off-campus visits with family as part of treatment — are scheduled by your treatment team when you're ready.

Clergy & Legal Counsel

You may see clergy of your choice and your attorney at any reasonable time. We'll provide a private space. These visits are not supervised by staff.

Family contact is a right, not a privilege. We will never take away phone calls, mail, or visits as a consequence for behavior. We will never tie family contact to “phases” or “levels.” If a specific contact needs to be limited for safety (for example, if a court order says no contact with a particular person), that's a clinical decision made with your guardian and your treatment team — not a punishment.

For Families
Court Hearings & How to Reach Your Child's Team
If your child's placement is involuntary or contested, a court hearing will be held within 15 days of admission so a judge can review the placement. An attorney will be appointed for your child at no cost to your family. You will be notified of the hearing date and may attend. To reach your child's treatment team, call the main facility line and ask for your child's therapist or case manager by name. For after-hours emergencies, an on-call Qualified Professional is available 24/7. Alliance Health (our regional managed care partner) Member Services can also help you navigate the system: 1-800-510-9132. You are not alone in this — please reach out whenever you need to.
Section 8
Consequences & Redirection
No consequences should ever place a child at risk of injury. — Healing Lodge of the Seven Nations

When you're off the mark, here's how we respond. The ladder goes from gentle to firm — we always start at the bottom and only move up if we need to.

  1. Verbal redirection. Staff gives you up to three prompts before assigning a formal consequence. “Hey, can you take a breath?” “Let's try that again.” Just a heads-up that you're drifting.
  2. “Take 5.” You can ask for — or staff can offer — a few minutes away from the situation to calm down and reset. Voluntary. Not a punishment. You choose when you're ready to come back.
  3. Refocus Time. Staff-directed brief reflection, up to 10 minutes. A chance to think about what happened and what you'd do differently.
  4. Reflection Time. Up to one hour in a designated area (not your room if it would be locked; never a “quiet room” used punitively). Time to write, think, or talk with staff about what's going on.
  5. Logical consequences. Repair or replace damaged property. Extra chores. A written reflection (never humiliation like writing “I will not talk” 500 times). Restitution through allowance or extra chores if something was damaged.
  6. Loss of specific privileges. TV, MP3 player, outings — implemented progressively (least restrictive first). Any restriction ends within 30 days unless reviewed and renewed in writing, with you present.
Forbidden — Never Used Here
No cruel or unusual punishment. No corporal punishment (hitting, spanking, grabbing). No deprivation of food, sleep, clothing, or shelter. No denial of education, religious practice, or family communication. No humiliation, intimidation, or degradation (no public apology as punishment, no “quiet room” as routine consequence). No group punishment for one youth's behavior. No running laps, push-ups, or physical exercise as punishment. No involuntary seclusion or restraint except in emergencies with imminent danger, physician order, time limits, and treatment-team review.

If you ever feel a consequence crosses the line into any of these forbidden categories, tell a staff member, file a grievance, or call Disability Rights NC. You will not be retaliated against for speaking up.

For Families
Our Commitment to Trauma-Informed Response
Our approach is modeled on the Healing Lodge of the Seven Nations' explicit statement that “no consequences should ever place a child at risk of injury” and that the facility “does not use physical restraint or seclusion.” We are working toward the Ukeru model — a comfort-based alternative to restraint and seclusion that trains staff to meet crisis behavior with relationship instead of force. All consequences are reviewed weekly by our Qualified Professional. Staff receive annual training in trauma-informed care, crisis de-escalation, and the principles that guide our no-harm promise. When your child is dysregulated, our first move is to be with them, not to do something to them.

Glossary — Words You'll Hear
Cardinal Rule — One of the five non-negotiable safety rules of our community. See Section 2.
Case Manager — The staff member who helps coordinate your care, appointments, school, and discharge planning.
Clinical Director — The person who oversees all therapy and clinical services at the facility.
Contraband — Items not allowed in the facility (weapons, drugs, lighters, etc.). See Section 6.
DHHS — NC Department of Health and Human Services. The state agency that oversees our license.
DHSR — NC Division of Health Service Regulation. The part of DHHS that inspects facilities like ours.
Disability Rights NC — North Carolina's Protection & Advocacy system. Independent. You can call them anytime: 1-877-235-4210.
Grievance — A formal complaint. You can file one about anything, without retaliation. See Section 1.
Joint Commission — An independent accrediting body that inspects hospitals and treatment facilities. You can call them anytime: 1-800-994-6610.
LME/MCO — Local Management Entity/Managed Care Organization. The regional agency that manages Medicaid-funded mental health services. Ours is Alliance Health.
Milieu — The living and learning environment of the facility. The “community” part of treatment.
Precautions — Temporary safety measures (like sharps precautions or elopement precautions) based on specific risks. Therapeutic, not punitive.
Program Director — The person who runs the day-to-day operations of the facility.
QP — Qualified Professional. A staff member with the training and credentials to provide clinical oversight.
Refocus Time — Staff-directed brief reflection period, up to 10 minutes. See Section 8.
Restraint — Physical holding of a resident. Used only in emergencies with imminent danger. Never a punishment.
Seclusion — Placing a resident alone in a locked room. Used only in emergencies. Never a punishment.
Strip Search — A search requiring removal of clothing. Prohibited here. Non-negotiable.
Therapeutic Pass — An approved off-campus visit with family or trusted support, as part of treatment.
TTV — Therapeutic Temporary Visit. A longer off-campus pass, usually a few hours or overnight, when you're ready.
Treatment Plan — Your individualized plan of care, written with you and your treatment team. Updated regularly.
Treatment Team — The group of people responsible for your care: therapist, case manager, psychiatrist, QP, primary staff, you, and your family.
Ukeru — A trauma-informed alternative to restraint, based on comfort and redirection. We are working toward this model.
